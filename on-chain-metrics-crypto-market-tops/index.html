On-Chain Metrics That Preceded the Last Three Crypto Market Tops

Ex-quant trader Marcus Chen breaks down the on-chain signals that flashed before Bitcoin's 2018, 2021, and 2022 tops. Numbers don't lie.

MC
Marcus Chen May 15, 2026 · Live coverage

Three weeks before Bitcoin peaked at $69,000 in November 2021, the MVRV Z-Score crossed above 7. That same indicator crossed above 7 in December 2017, roughly two weeks before the $19,800 top. When I first backtested this in 2020, I thought it was too clean to be real. It wasn't a coincidence. On-chain data leaves fingerprints, and those fingerprints have been remarkably consistent across the last three major cycle tops.

Ex-quant trader Marcus Chen breaks down the on-chain signals that flashed before Bitcoin's 2018, 2021, and 2022 tops. Numbers don't lie.

MVRV Z-Score and Unrealized Profit Saturation

The MVRV Z-Score measures the ratio of Bitcoin's market cap to its realized cap, then normalizes that against historical standard deviation. When it breaches 7, the market is holding an historically extreme level of unrealized profit. Historically, that creates a fragile structure. Holders who bought lower have enormous incentive to sell, and new buyers are absorbing that supply at prices that can't be justified by Jordan Osei.

In late November 2017, the Z-Score hit approximately 9.5 before the December top. In October and November 2021, it peaked around 7.2, which was actually lower than 2017 but still well inside the danger zone. The 2022 local top in November was a different beast, a bear market relief rally, and the Z-Score only reached around 2.1, which is why I'd argue that top was more about macro pressure than on-chain excess. The metric worked differently there, and that distinction matters.

Research from Glassnode published in 2021 documented how MVRV readings above 3.7 have historically corresponded with periods of elevated sell pressure. The zone above 7 has preceded every major cycle top since 2013. That's a small sample size, and I won't pretend otherwise, but it's a consistent one.

Exchange Inflows Spiking as Smart Money Exits

One of the clearest signals before both the 2018 and 2021 tops was a sharp increase in the volume of Bitcoin flowing onto exchanges. When long-term holders move coins to exchanges, they're almost always preparing to sell. It's a behavioral tell. In the two weeks before the January 2018 top, exchange inflow volume jumped by roughly 40% relative to the 30-day average. The same pattern appeared in October and early November 2021.

But here's what most people miss: you need to separate the exchange inflow signal from raw volume. During bull runs, total volume rises. What you're watching for is the ratio of exchange inflows to total transferred volume. When that ratio spikes while price is still climbing, it means distribution is occurring into strength. In my work with systematic momentum strategies between 2019 and 2021, this ratio consistently flagged tops 10 to 18 days before price reversed.

The 2022 top showed a muted version of this. Inflows rose, but the absolute magnitude was smaller, which tracks with the idea that 2022 was a secondary top rather than a full cycle peak. The signal was there, just quieter. That's an honest limitation of the metric: it scales with cycle intensity, so you need to contextualize it against the specific phase of the market you're in.

Long-Term Holder Supply Compression Before Each Peak

Long-term holders, defined by Glassnode as addresses holding for more than 155 days, tend to accumulate through bear markets and distribute into bull markets. The percentage of supply held by this cohort peaked and then began declining approximately 30 to 60 days before each of the last three cycle tops. Before the December 2017 peak, long-term holder supply had been falling since late October. Before November 2021, the decline started in September.

This matters because long-term holders are the most informed cohort in the market. They've survived at least one prior downturn and they're not panic selling. When they start moving coins, they're making a deliberate decision about valuation. A sustained drop in their aggregate supply is the market quietly telling you that the people who understand cycles best are cashing out.

There's a common misconception here that I want to address directly. Many retail analysts treat any drop in long-term holder supply as bearish. It's not. Small, temporary dips happen throughout a bull run as some holders take partial profits. What you're watching for is a sustained, multi-week decline that accelerates as price rises. That divergence, supply dropping while price climbs, is the actual warning. A single week of outflows means very little on its own.

Funding Rates and Perpetual Open Interest as the Final Warning

By the time MVRV and exchange inflows are flashing, the derivatives market usually confirms the picture. Perpetual futures funding rates measure whether longs or shorts are paying to hold positions. When funding rates run persistently above 0.1% per 8-hour period for more than two weeks, the market is overleveraged on the long side. That was true before the May 2021 correction, before the November 2021 top, and before the June 2022 collapse after the LUNA disaster.

I've seen this pattern play out enough times to take it seriously. Before the November 2021 top, annualized funding rates on Binance and FTX averaged somewhere between 60% and 80% for the preceding three weeks. That means longs were paying shorts an annualized rate approaching 80% just to stay in their positions. That's not bullish conviction. That's crowded speculation. And crowded speculation unwinds fast when price stalls.

Open interest as a percentage of market cap is the complementary signal. Before the April 2021 top, open interest on Bitcoin perpetuals reached approximately 2.3% of total market cap. Before November 2021, it was closer to 1.9%. Both of those readings represented extreme leverage relative to spot market size. The 2022 local top showed similar but compressed dynamics. So the metric works, but the thresholds shift slightly with each cycle as the derivatives market matures and gains more institutional participation. That's an area of genuine ongoing debate among quants: whether fixed thresholds are meaningful or whether you need rolling normalization.

The takeaway

The takeaway

The one actionable takeaway is this: don't watch any single metric in isolation. The framework that held across 2018, 2021, and 2022 was a convergence, MVRV Z-Score above 7, sustained long-term holder supply decline, elevated exchange inflow ratios, and persistent high funding rates all appearing within the same 30-day window. When three or four of those conditions align simultaneously, that's the moment to reassess position sizing and take partial profits. Not because the top is confirmed, but because the asymmetry has shifted. The reward for staying fully exposed gets smaller while the risk gets larger. That's not fear, it's math.
